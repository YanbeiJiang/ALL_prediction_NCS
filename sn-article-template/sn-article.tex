%% Abstract

\abstract{%
Prediction models for paediatric acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL)
are usually trained on a hospital's own historical cases, yet a
hospital that adopts a new protocol starts with none.
Here we ask how much local data a zero-shot large language model (LLM)
is worth along the clinical timeline.
We aligned six nested feature sets with data acquisition from
admission to the completion of diagnostic work-up (Day~7--10), before
any assessment of minimal residual disease (MRD), and predicted six
decisions from the last of them—Final Risk, day-19 and day-46 MRD,
relapse, event-free survival (EFS) and overall survival (OS).
Random forests and random survival forests were trained on cases
partitioned by enrolment year (301 patients, 2015--2019), with an
external cohort of 41 patients from two hospitals.
An equal-weight ensemble of five zero-shot LLMs matched random forests
trained on about 50 local patients for Final Risk and 100 for day-19
MRD (AUROC 0.93 and 0.78; differences from fully trained forests not
significant).
For EFS and OS, locally trained random survival forests did not reach
the ensemble at any training size (C-index 0.59 and 0.62 with all 186
training patients, versus 0.68 and 0.71).
At the external hospitals the survival forests lost discrimination
(EFS 0.44, OS 0.51) whereas the LLM ensemble did not (0.69 and 0.84;
$P<0.01$ for both, and $P<0.05$ when only patients from the same
hospital were compared).
Final Risk was largely fixed by Initial Risk, which alone reproduced it
in 90.7\% of patients, and relapse and day-46 MRD remained near chance
for all methods.
Zero-shot LLMs are therefore a credible starting point before local
data accrue, and remain complementary afterwards.
}

\keywords{paediatric acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, large language
models, zero-shot prediction, learning curves, temporal validation,
survival analysis, external validation}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%%==============================================================%%


Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL) is the most common childhood
malignancy, accounting for approximately 25\% of all paediatric
cancers~\cite{[CITE:Hunger2015]}.
Risk-adapted chemotherapy has elevated five-year event-free survival
(EFS) rates above 90\% in high-income settings, yet the 15--20\% of
patients who relapse face substantially worse outcomes, with long-term
survival dropping to 30--50\% upon re-induction~\cite{[CITE:Bhojwani2013],[CITE:Pui2008]}.
Because the majority of relapses occur after apparent clinical
remission, the prospective identification of high-risk patients—early
enough to intensify or de-escalate therapy—remains a central and
unresolved challenge in paediatric oncology.

Current standard-of-care risk stratification—Initial Risk at
diagnosis and Final Risk after the Day~46 induction assessment—relies
on pre-specified categorical cut-offs for age, white blood cell count,
cytogenetics, immunophenotype and minimal residual disease (MRD),
established in large cooperative-group
studies~\cite{[CITE:Schultz2007],[CITE:Borowitz2008],[CITE:Conter2010]}.
These rules do not yield personalised probability estimates for future
relapse or survival, and a growing literature has sought to provide
them: Cox-regression nomograms~\cite{[CITE:Mao2020],[CITE:Zhang2020],[CITE:He2022],[CITE:Sang2026]},
risk scores and association studies built on the day-19 and day-46 MRD
time points of the CCCG-ALL-2015 protocol~\cite{[CITE:Liu2022TALL],[CITE:Chen2025FrontPediatr]},
machine-learning models on routine clinical
variables~\cite{[CITE:Pan2017],[CITE:Mehrbakhsh2024],[CITE:Song2025],[CITE:Cui2025Transformer],[CITE:Saravanan2026]},
and models built on gene-expression, single-cell, exome, methylation
or RNA-sequencing profiles~\cite{[CITE:Kang2010],[CITE:Fitter2021],[CITE:Good2018],[CITE:Bohannan2022],[CITE:Mosquera2024],[CITE:Zhai2026]}.
Relapse has proved hardest: computational flow cytometry at diagnosis
failed to predict it~\cite{[CITE:Martinez2025]}, and the largest
machine-learning relapse model reached 68.5\% sensitivity at 50.3\%
specificity~\cite{[CITE:Saravanan2026]}.

Most of these models make simplifications that break down at the
bedside (Table~\ref{tab:related}).
They typically predict one endpoint at one decision point, and they are
validated by random split or cross-validation: a systematic review
found external validation in only 5 of 20 machine-learning models for
paediatric haematological malignancies~\cite{[CITE:Gurumurthy2025]}, a
meta-analysis of childhood-cancer prognosis models reported that no
study had yet assessed AI-based models in haematological
malignancies~\cite{[CITE:Varga2024]}, and none of the paediatric ALL
studies we identified used a temporal split by enrolment year.
The work closest to ours, clinTALL~\cite{[CITE:Stoiber2026]}, jointly
predicts T-ALL subtypes, competing events and EFS with a multi-task
network restricted to pre-induction variables, but it does not organise
features along the acquisition timeline and did not quantitatively
evaluate outcome prediction on its external cohorts.

We argue that realistic clinical prediction is governed by three
distinct and largely independent timelines, and that existing
paediatric ALL models address them at most partially.
The first is the \emph{feature-acquisition timeline}: a patient's data
do not arrive at once but in a fixed clinical order—white blood cell
count and age at admission (Day~0), immunophenotype (Day~1),
fusion genes, karyotype and FISH (Day~7--10), after which treatment
response is measured as MRD on Days~19 and 46.
A model trained on features pooled across all timepoints implicitly
uses information unavailable at the moment of decision, producing
optimistic but non-deployable estimates~\cite{[CITE:Steyerberg2019]}.
The second is the \emph{prediction-target timeline}: clinicians do not
make one prediction but a sequence of decisions—early response
(D19MRD), end-of-induction response (D46MRD), Final Risk assignment,
and long-horizon outcomes (relapse, EFS, overall survival [OS]).
Studying any single target in isolation, as is common, obscures how one
data-acquisition workflow must support all six decision nodes.
The third is the \emph{data-accumulation timeline}: a hospital's
reference database grows year by year, so a model deployed in a given
year can only have learned from patients enrolled before it.
Random train--test splits ignore this constraint, so they cannot show
how a model behaves as a hospital's database grows.

The third timeline exposes a practical problem that the first two do
not: a hospital that adopts a new protocol, or a centre that begins to
collect structured data, starts with no local cases to learn from.
Large language models (LLMs) can be queried zero-shot, drawing on
clinical knowledge acquired during pretraining~\cite{[CITE:Phaterpekar2026]}.
On adult hospital data, language models have predicted readmission,
mortality and other outcomes after domain pretraining, with temporal
and prospective validation~\cite{[CITE:Jiang2023NYUTron]}, or zero-shot
from tokenised patient timelines~\cite{[CITE:Renc2024ETHOS]}, and
LLM-extracted features from clinical notes have improved survival
models in adult cancer~\cite{[CITE:Kiermeyer2026]}.
With serialised structured data, however, the value of prompted LLMs
depends on the comparator: they have often outperformed conventional
models trained on ten examples~\cite{[CITE:Zhu2026ClinicRealm]} yet
fallen well short of fully trained
ones~\cite{[CITE:ClinicalBench2026],[CITE:Brown2025JAMIA]}.
Retrieval of similar patients or of external knowledge can
help~\cite{[CITE:ChenPSB2026],[CITE:Datta2026],[CITE:Xu2024RAMEHR],[CITE:Zhu2024EMERGE],[CITE:Chen2025JMIR]},
but none of these studies concerns paediatric oncology.
It therefore remains unclear how much local data a zero-shot LLM is
worth, for which decisions it suffices, and whether it remains useful
once a case database has accrued or when a model is moved to another
hospital.
We address these questions on a dataset in which six temporally
ordered clinical feature sets (five strictly nested; $\mathcal{F}_4$ is
a Day-1 branch of $\mathcal{F}_3$) map onto the feature-acquisition
timeline up to the completion of diagnostic work-up, six prediction
targets map onto the target timeline, and enrolment year defines the
data-accumulation timeline.
Every target is predicted before the first MRD assessment, so MRD is
always an outcome and never an input.
We compare an ensemble of five zero-shot LLMs (Phase~1) with decision
trees, random forests and random survival forests trained on the
growing case database under temporal splits (Phase~2), test both on an
external cohort from two hospitals without retraining, and add a
retrieval-based belief revision that uses the accumulated cases to
revise model probabilities and to ground LLM-generated clinical
narratives.

Our contributions are threefold.
(\textit{i})~A timeline-aligned evaluation of six clinical decisions in
paediatric ALL that shows when each becomes predictable before
treatment response is known: Final Risk is largely fixed by Initial
Risk at Day~5, day-19 MRD becomes predictable once immunophenotype is
available, and relapse and day-46 MRD remain near chance with routine
data.
(\textit{ii})~A quantification of the value of zero-shot LLMs relative
to accumulating local data: the LLM ensemble matched random forests
trained on about 50 and 100 local patients for Final Risk and day-19
MRD, was not reached by locally trained survival forests at any
training size for EFS and OS, and kept its survival discrimination at
two external hospitals where the trained models lost theirs.
(\textit{iii})~A retrieval-based belief revision extended to censored
survival outcomes, with LLM-generated narratives for physician review.

\subsection{Cohorts and timeline-aligned design}
\label{sec:cohort}

The internal cohort comprised 301 children with ALL treated at
[HOSPITAL NAME] between 2015 and 2019 (Table~\ref{tab:cohort}).
Prediction targets span the treatment timeline: Day~19 MRD positivity
(D19 MRD $\geq 1\%$), Day~46 MRD positivity (D46 MRD $\geq 0.01\%$),
Final Risk (LR versus IR/HR; 159 LR, 142 IR/HR), relapse (44 of 301,
14.6\%), event-free survival (EFS; relapse, induction failure or death;
75 events, 24.9\%) and overall survival (OS; 24 deaths, 8.0\%).
The external cohort pooled 41 patients from two hospitals: Lianyungang
First People's Hospital (20 patients, diagnosed 2019--2023) and
Children's Hospital of Fudan University, Shanghai (21 patients,
2017--2021).
The Fudan patients form a relapse-enriched referral series in which
every patient relapsed, so the pooled external relapse rate (23 of 41)
is far above that of an unselected ALL population.
No external data were used for training.

Figure~\ref{fig:overview} summarises the design.
Six feature levels $\mathcal{F}_1$--$\mathcal{F}_6$ follow the order
in which data become available, from white blood cell count and age at
admission to fusion genes, karyotype and FISH at Day~7--10 (variable
list in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:featurelist}).
All six targets are predicted at the pre-specified decision level
$\mathcal{F}_6$, the last level available before the Day-19 MRD
assessment.
MRD values are therefore never used as inputs, and every prediction is
made before treatment response is known, when therapy could still be
adapted.
At each level we compare an equal-weight ensemble of five zero-shot
LLMs (GPT-6.1-Sol, Gemini-4-Argon, Gemma-4-31B-it, InternVL3.5-30B-A3B
and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B), which uses no training data, with random forests
(RF) and random survival forests (RSF) trained on patients from earlier
enrolment years.
The primary internal test set is the 2018--19 enrolment cohort
(Split~2; 186 training and 114 test patients); the other two temporal
splits are reported in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:supp_decision}.
Discrimination is summarised by AUROC for classification, where the
LLM ensemble's score is the fraction of the five models predicting the
positive class, and by Harrell's C-index for survival.

%%--------------------------------------------------------------%%
\subsection{What becomes predictable, and when}
\label{sec:when}
%%--------------------------------------------------------------%%

Figure~\ref{fig:when} shows discrimination for every target at every
feature level, in the internal test set and at the external hospitals.
Three patterns stand out.

\textbf{Final Risk is largely fixed by Initial Risk.}
Trained RF reached an AUROC of 0.87 from white blood cell count and age
alone and 0.94--0.95 once Initial Risk entered at $\mathcal{F}_5$; the
LLM ensemble rose from 0.79 to 0.93 over the same levels.
This follows from how Final Risk is defined.
In our cohort Initial Risk alone reproduced Final Risk in 90.7\% of
internal patients, and a three-condition rule—Initial Risk of IR or HR,
D19 MRD $\geq 1\%$, or D46 MRD $\geq 0.01\%$—reproduced it in 299 of
301 internal patients (99.3\%) and in 40 of 41 external patients.
At $\mathcal{F}_6$ both approaches performed close to Initial Risk
alone (balanced accuracy 0.901 for the LLM ensemble and 0.891 for the
RF, versus 0.906 in the 2018--19 test set).
The remaining uncertainty is which of the 187 initially low-risk
patients will be upgraded (28 patients, 26 of them MRD-positive), which
is the MRD prediction problem.

\textbf{D19 MRD becomes predictable with immunophenotype.}
RF AUROC was 0.42 and 0.51 at $\mathcal{F}_1$ and $\mathcal{F}_2$ and
0.74--0.81 from $\mathcal{F}_3$, when immunophenotype and flow
cytometry markers entered; the LLM ensemble reached 0.68--0.69 at
$\mathcal{F}_3$ and $\mathcal{F}_4$ and 0.78--0.79 from
$\mathcal{F}_5$.

\textbf{Before MRD, survival discrimination comes mainly from the LLMs.}
The RSF C-index stayed between 0.53 and 0.61 for EFS and between 0.50
and 0.62 for OS at every level.
The LLM ensemble reached 0.71 for OS from Day~5, once Initial Risk was
available, and 0.68 for EFS at $\mathcal{F}_6$, when cytogenetics
entered.
Without MRD, which dominates prognosis in contemporary
protocols~\cite{[CITE:Borowitz2008],[CITE:Conter2010]}, the trained
models had little signal to learn from local events, whereas the LLMs
could draw on prior knowledge of how the diagnostic features relate to
outcome.

\textbf{Relapse and D46 MRD remain close to chance.}
Neither approach exceeded an AUROC of 0.68 for either target at any
level.
Results for each LLM and each level are given in Supplementary
Tables~\ref{tab:phase1_full}--\ref{tab:phase2_ext}.

%%--------------------------------------------------------------%%
\subsection{How many local patients is a zero-shot LLM worth?}
\label{sec:worth}
%%--------------------------------------------------------------%%

To quantify the value of zero-shot LLMs relative to local data, we
retrained RF and RSF on random subsets of $m$ patients from the
2015--17 training years and evaluated them on the same 2018--19 test
patients as the LLM ensemble (Fig.~\ref{fig:worth}; 20 repeats per
size).
The LLM ensemble matched trained models built from about 49 patients
for Final Risk and 101 for D19 MRD.
For EFS and OS, the RSF stayed below the LLM ensemble at every training
size, including all 186 training patients (mean C-index at most 0.61
for EFS and 0.62 for OS, against 0.68 and 0.71).

With all training data (Table~\ref{tab:main}), no internal difference
between the LLM ensemble and trained models was statistically
significant.
Trained RF had slightly higher AUROC for Final Risk (0.950 versus
0.932; difference $-0.017$, 95\% CI $-0.066$ to $0.024$) and D19 MRD
(0.811 versus 0.784), whereas the LLM ensemble had higher C-indices for
EFS (0.682 versus 0.592; $+0.090$, $-0.008$ to $0.191$; $p=0.071$) and
OS (0.713 versus 0.620; $+0.093$, $-0.043$ to $0.245$).
The survival advantage pointed the same way in the earlier split
trained on 2015--16 (EFS $+0.049$, OS $+0.059$) and, for EFS, in the
2019 test set ($+0.066$; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:supp_decision}).
The OS training sets contained only 8 and 13 deaths, so a model that
must learn from local events has little to learn from, whereas the
LLMs bring prognostic knowledge from pretraining.

For Final Risk, neither approach went beyond Initial Risk.
The LLM ensemble and the RF assigned the correct group with balanced
accuracies of 0.901 and 0.891, against 0.906 for Initial Risk alone;
the remaining errors are patients upgraded on the basis of MRD, which
no model can know at $\mathcal{F}_6$.
The protocol rule reaches 1.000 only because it uses the Day-19 and
Day-46 MRD results.

\subsection{Retrieval-based belief revision and clinical narratives}
\label{sec:rag}
%%--------------------------------------------------------------%%

Revising RF probabilities towards the outcomes of the five most similar
training cases (Methods) changed classification AUROC by $-0.049$ to
$+0.073$ across tasks and splits, apart from a gain of $+0.226$ for D46
MRD in the 17-patient 2019 test set, which contains only three
positives (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:belief_revision}).
For survival, a censoring-aware variant that replaces neighbour
outcomes with a similarity-weighted Kaplan--Meier event probability
gave small but consistent gains (mean C-index change $+0.007$ over 42
combinations of feature level $\mathcal{F}_1$--$\mathcal{F}_6$, test
set and endpoint, 26 of them positive; Wilcoxon $p=0.003$).
For each test patient, an LLM then produced a short narrative combining
the prediction, the decision-tree path and the retrieved cases; a
structured physician evaluation of these narratives is under way
(Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:narrative_eval}).

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
%%==============================================================%%

Most prediction models in paediatric ALL assume that a hospital's own
historical cases are available for training.
By aligning six clinical decisions with the timelines along which data
are acquired and accumulate, we found that this assumption matters
less than expected for several of them.
Predicting every decision at the end of diagnostic work-up, before any
MRD result, a zero-shot ensemble of five LLMs using no local data
matched random forests trained on about 50 and 100 local patients for
Final Risk and D19 MRD.
For EFS and OS it was not reached by survival forests trained on any
available number of local patients, and it kept its discrimination at
two other hospitals, where the trained survival models fell to or below
chance.

\textbf{When a zero-shot LLM suffices.}
These results help reconcile two apparently conflicting lines of
evidence.
Prompted LLMs have outperformed conventional models trained on ten
examples~\cite{[CITE:Zhu2026ClinicRealm]} yet fallen well short of
models trained on full datasets~\cite{[CITE:ClinicalBench2026],[CITE:Brown2025JAMIA]}.
Our learning curves locate the crossover within a single disease: about
50 patients for Final Risk, around 100 for D19 MRD, and beyond the
available data for EFS and OS, where only 8 to 13 deaths were
available for training.
The LLMs' advantage is largest where local events are scarce and where
the case mix shifts, the two situations in which a locally trained
model has least to learn from or most to unlearn.
In practice, a centre starting a new protocol could use a zero-shot LLM
ensemble for survival risk from the first patient, and move to, or
combine it with, locally trained models for the classification targets
once 50 to 100 cases have accrued.

\textbf{Where it does not.}
For the classification targets, the LLM ensemble offered no advantage
over trained models once a modest case database existed, and for Final
Risk neither approach went beyond Initial Risk alone.
Decisions that are defined by a stratification protocol are better
implemented as explicit logic than predicted by a language model.
The value of zero-shot LLMs lies instead in outcomes that no rule
defines—survival and, to a lesser extent, early treatment response.

\textbf{MRD and the limits of routine data.}
Because all predictions were made before treatment response was
measured, they test what can be anticipated from diagnostic data alone.
In this setting the trained survival forests stayed at C-indices of
0.50 to 0.62, consistent with the established role of MRD, which our
design withholds, as the dominant prognostic
factor~\cite{[CITE:Borowitz2008],[CITE:Conter2010]}; the LLM ensemble
reached 0.68 to 0.71, suggesting that prior knowledge of how Initial
Risk and cytogenetics relate to outcome can partly substitute for
local events.
Relapse and D46 MRD remained near chance at every level, consistent
with the failure of computational flow cytometry at
diagnosis~\cite{[CITE:Martinez2025]} and the modest discrimination of
a large clinical relapse model~\cite{[CITE:Saravanan2026]}.
Stronger relapse signal has so far come mainly from single-cell and
molecular profiles~\cite{[CITE:Good2018],[CITE:Mosquera2024]}, which
our routine feature set does not include; adding such data at the
appropriate point of the timeline is a natural next step.

\textbf{Retrieval-based revision.}
Revising model probabilities with similar past cases gave small,
consistent gains for survival but mixed results for classification.
Retrieval helps when the feature space clusters by outcome and
neighbours agree, and adds noise when they do not, as for relapse.
A practical safeguard would be to revise only when neighbour outcomes
agree beyond a calibrated threshold.

\textbf{Limitations.}
The internal cohort comes from a single institution, is smaller than
the cooperative-group and registry cohorts behind earlier risk
models~\cite{[CITE:Schultz2007],[CITE:He2022],[CITE:Sang2026]}, and was
analysed retrospectively; prospective deployment, as reported for a
clinical language model in adult care~\cite{[CITE:Jiang2023NYUTron]},
remains a stronger standard of validation than our temporal and
external evaluation.
The external cohort is small (41 patients) and includes a
relapse-enriched referral series, so external relapse and MRD results
are not interpretable and the confidence intervals of the survival
differences are wide; larger, prospectively collected external cohorts
are needed to confirm the robustness of the LLM ensemble.
Internally, the survival advantage of the LLM ensemble did not reach
statistical significance in any single test set.
Learning curves were estimated on one temporal split, the 2019 test set
contains only 17 patients, and no correction was made for multiple
comparisons.
All predictions were made before MRD assessment; we did not study how
they should be updated once MRD results become available.
The LLMs were used zero-shot with fixed prompts; their outputs may
change with model versions, the two proprietary models were queried
with their providers' default decoding settings and are therefore not
strictly deterministic, and fine-tuning or few-shot prompting might
improve the classification targets.
Because each LLM returns a hard label for classification, the
ensemble's classification score has only six levels, which limits its
AUROC.
Finally, physician evaluation of the LLM narratives is in progress and
will determine whether they are useful beyond illustrative case
studies.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}
%%==============================================================%%

\subsection{Datasets and ethics}\label{sec:dataset}

The internal training cohort comprised $n=301$ patients diagnosed with
ALL at [HOSPITAL NAME] between 2015 and 2019.
All patients were treated with risk-stratified chemotherapy protocols
consistent with contemporary Chinese paediatric oncology guidelines.
Ethics approval was obtained from [ETHICS NUMBER]; all data were
de-identified prior to analysis.
Each patient record includes structured clinical data (demographics,
haematological indices, immunophenotype, 51-marker flow cytometry panel,
cytogenetics, MRD values).

The external validation cohort ($n=41$) was supplied as a single
merged file spanning two source hospitals, distinguished by the
recorded hospital field: Lianyungang First People's Hospital (20
patients, 2019--2023) and Children's Hospital of Fudan University,
Shanghai (21 patients, 2017--2021, a relapse-enriched referral cohort
in which all patients relapsed); ethics approval: [ETHICS NUMBERS,
external cohort]. We pool the two hospitals and evaluate the external
cohort as a single group throughout (Section~\ref{sec:ood}).
No external data were used in any model training.

\subsection{Temporally-structured feature hierarchy}\label{sec:features}

Feature sets are temporally ordered and, except for the
$\mathcal{F}_4$ branch, form a strictly nested sequence
$\mathcal{F}_1 \subseteq \mathcal{F}_2 \subseteq \mathcal{F}_3
\subseteq \mathcal{F}_5 \subseteq \mathcal{F}_6$
($\mathcal{F}_4$ branches from $\mathcal{F}_3$; Table~\ref{tab:featurelist}),
where each level contains exactly the features available at the
corresponding clinical timepoint.
Boundaries: $\mathcal{F}_1$~(Day~0): WBC count and age;
$\mathcal{F}_2$~(Day~1): adds blast percentage;
$\mathcal{F}_3$~(Day~1): adds immunophenotype and 51 flow cytometry markers;
$\mathcal{F}_4$~(Day~1): adds non-risk clinical factors;
$\mathcal{F}_5$~(Day~5): adds CNS status, Day~5 blast, and Initial Risk;
$\mathcal{F}_6$~(Day~7--10): adds Fusion gene, Karyotype, FISH.
The complete variable list for every level is given in
Table~\ref{tab:featurelist}.
D19 and D46 MRD are prediction targets and are not used as input
features at any level.
All six prediction targets are evaluated on the same feature hierarchy.

For Phase~2, categorical features were one-hot encoded and numerical
features z-score normalised using training-split statistics only:
\begin{equation}
z_{ij}^{(\text{test})} =
\frac{x_{ij}^{(\text{test})} - \mu_j^{(\text{train})}}
     {\sigma_j^{(\text{train})}},
\label{eq:zscore}
\end{equation}
with missing values imputed by training-split medians.

\subsection{Phase~1: LLM zero-shot inference}\label{sec:llm_inference}

For classification tasks, each patient's features were serialised into a
key--value list and presented with a task-specific system prompt
requesting a bracketed label (e.g., \texttt{[[IR]]}/\texttt{[[LR]]}).
For survival risk scoring, the same serialised features were presented
with a prompt requesting a continuous event probability (0.0--1.0).
Five LLMs were used: two proprietary models accessed through their
providers' APIs, GPT-6.1-Sol (OpenAI) and Gemini-4-Argon (Google), and
three open-weight models, Gemma-4-31B-it, InternVL3.5-30B-A3B and
Qwen3.6-35B-A3B.
The open-weight models were served with vLLM~\cite{[CITE:vLLM]} at
temperature~$0$ (greedy decoding; Qwen3.6 with its thinking mode
disabled).
The proprietary models were queried with the providers' default
decoding settings (Gemini-4-Argon on 30--31 October 2026 and
GPT-6.1-Sol on 31 October 2026).
Because each classification output is a single hard label,
classification performance is summarised by balanced accuracy,
$\mathrm{BA}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathrm{TPR}+\mathrm{TNR})$, which equals
the AUROC of the binary prediction.
The five LLMs were combined into an ensemble with equal weights and no
tuning.
For classification, the ensemble score is the fraction of models
predicting the positive class (patients without a valid answer from
any model were excluded), and the ensemble label is positive when at
least three of the five models agree.
For survival, the ensemble score is the mean of the five predicted
event probabilities; the mean within-cohort rank of the five scores
was used as a sensitivity analysis (Supplementary
Table~\ref{tab:supp_decision}).
Per-patient outputs were cached; the cohort was scored for every
combination of feature level ($\times 6$), task ($\times 6$), and
model ($\times 5$).

\subsection{Phase~2: Decision tree and random forest}\label{sec:dt}

A decision tree (DT; \texttt{max\_depth}=4, \texttt{class\_weight}=%
`balanced', \texttt{criterion}=`gini', \texttt{random\_state}=42)
and a random forest (RF; $n_\text{estimators}=100$,
\texttt{class\_weight}=`balanced') were trained for each feature level
and temporal split.
Three temporal splits defined by enrolment year threshold $\mathcal{T}_s$
(Eq.~\ref{eq:splits}) ensure strict temporal non-leakage.
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{D}_s^{\text{train}} = \{i : \tau_i \leq \mathcal{T}_s\},
\quad
\mathcal{D}_s^{\text{test}} = \{i : \tau_i > \mathcal{T}_s\}.
\label{eq:splits}
\end{equation}
Enrolment year $\tau_i$ is taken from the diagnostic smear date; one
patient whose date could not be parsed is excluded from all splits.
Patients with a missing label for a task are excluded from that task's
training and test sets.
Primary metric: AUROC; secondary: F1-macro and balanced accuracy.

\subsection{Phase~2: Random survival forest}\label{sec:rsf}

A random survival forest (RSF;~\cite{[CITE:Ishwaran2008]};
$n_\text{estimators}=100$, \texttt{random\_state}=42) was trained on
the same temporal splits as the classification experiments; a single
survival decision tree (survival-tree analogue of the classification DT)
was trained under the same protocol as its tree-based counterpart.
EFS was defined as time from diagnosis to first of: relapse, induction
failure, or death; OS was time to death.
Censoring was at last follow-up.
Evaluation used Harrell's C-index (Eq.~\ref{eq:cindex}), IPCW
time-dependent AUC (Eq.~\ref{eq:itauc}) at $t^*\in\{12,24,36\}$
months, and the log-rank test on Kaplan-Meier curves dichotomised at
the median risk score.

\subsection{External OOD evaluation}\label{sec:ood}

For external validation, all $n=301$ internal patients served as the
training set and external patients served as the test set; no
site-specific fine-tuning was performed.
DT and RF were trained on the full internal set using the same
preprocessing pipeline (Eq.~\ref{eq:zscore}).
RSF was trained identically.
For Phase~1 LLM evaluation, no training was involved; the same
zero-shot prompts were applied to external patient feature vectors.

\subsection{Decision levels, learning curves and protocol baseline}\label{sec:comparison}

Before comparing methods, we fixed a single decision level for all
targets: $\mathcal{F}_6$, the last level before the Day-19 MRD
assessment.
D19 and D46 MRD are thus predicted before they are measured, and Final
Risk, relapse, EFS and OS are predicted before treatment response is
known, when the intensity of subsequent therapy could still be adapted.
All comparisons between the LLM ensemble and trained models use this
level and the same test patients.
For balanced accuracy, RF probabilities were thresholded at the
training-set prevalence.
Learning curves were computed on Split~2: for
$m \in \{10, 20, 40, 60, 80, 120, 160\}$ and for the full training set,
RF or RSF models with the settings above were trained on $m$ patients
drawn without replacement from the 2015--17 enrolment years (20 repeats
per $m$) and evaluated on the 2018--19 test patients with a valid LLM
prediction.
The LLM-equivalent training size is the value of $m$ at which the mean
learning curve first reaches the LLM ensemble, obtained by linear
interpolation between adjacent sizes.
As references for Final Risk we used Initial Risk alone (LR versus
IR/HR), which is available at $\mathcal{F}_5$, and a protocol rule that
assigned IR/HR when Initial Risk was IR or HR, D19 MRD was $\geq 1\%$,
or D46 MRD was $\geq 0.01\%$; because the rule requires MRD, it is not
available at the decision level and serves only to show how Final Risk
is defined.

\subsection{RAG belief revision and LLM narration}\label{sec:rag_methods}

For test patient $i$, the $K=5$ most similar training cases were
retrieved by cosine similarity in the standardised feature space:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{N}(i) = \underset{j \in \mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}}
  {\arg\text{top-}K}\
  \frac{\mathbf{z}_i \cdot \mathbf{z}_j}
       {\|\mathbf{z}_i\|_2 \|\mathbf{z}_j\|_2}, \quad K = 5.
\label{eq:rag}
\end{equation}
The RAG evidence probability is
\begin{equation}
p_{\text{rag}} =
  \frac{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} s_j\, y_j}
       {\sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} s_j},
\label{eq:prag}
\end{equation}
where $s_j$ is the cosine similarity to neighbour $j$ and
$y_j \in \{0,1\}$ is the binary task outcome (or event indicator
for survival).
The revised probability is
\begin{equation}
p_{\text{rev}} = \alpha\, p_0 + (1-\alpha)\, p_{\text{rag}},
\qquad
\alpha = 1 - (1-\alpha_{\text{base}})\sqrt{\bar{s}},
\label{eq:prev}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_\text{base}=0.6$ and
$\bar{s} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} s_j$.
For survival, $p_0$ is the min-max normalised RSF risk score.

After belief revision, an LLM prompt containing the patient's features,
the human-readable DT decision path~\cite{[CITE:Rudin2019]}, top-6 salient features (ranked by
$\text{importance}_j \times |z_{ij}|$), and top-5 retrieved case
profiles was submitted to Gemini-3.1-Flash-Lite to generate a 3--5
sentence clinical narrative.

\subsection{Survival metrics}\label{sec:surv_methods}

Harrell's concordance index:
\begin{equation}
\hat{C} =
\frac{
  \sum_{i \neq j}
  \mathbf{1}[T_i < T_j]\,
  \mathbf{1}[\delta_i = 1]\,
  \mathbf{1}[\hat{r}_i > \hat{r}_j]
}{
  \sum_{i \neq j}
  \mathbf{1}[T_i < T_j]\,
  \mathbf{1}[\delta_i = 1]
}.
\label{eq:cindex}
\end{equation}
IPCW time-dependent AUC:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\text{AUC}}(t^*) =
\frac{
  \sum_{i:\,T_i \leq t^*,\,\delta_i=1}
  \sum_{j:\,T_j > t^*}
  \mathbf{1}[\hat{r}_i > \hat{r}_j]\,\hat{w}_i
}{
  \sum_{i:\,T_i \leq t^*,\,\delta_i=1}
  \sum_{j:\,T_j > t^*}
  \hat{w}_i
},
\label{eq:itauc}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{w}_i = 1/\hat{G}(T_i)^2$ and $\hat{G}(\cdot)$ is the
Kaplan-Meier censoring estimator~\cite{[CITE:Uno2011]}.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}

AUROC and balanced accuracy were computed with
\texttt{scikit-learn}~\cite{[CITE:sklearn]}, and C-index and IPCW AUC
with \texttt{scikit-survival}~\cite{[CITE:sksurv]}.
Confidence intervals are percentile intervals from 2,000 patient-level
bootstrap resamples.
Differences between the LLM ensemble and trained models were computed
on the same resamples, with two-sided $p$-values given by twice the
smaller tail of the bootstrap distribution of the difference.
In the external cohort we also computed a within-hospital C-index that
counts only patient pairs from the same hospital, with bootstrap
resampling stratified by hospital.
Kaplan--Meier curves were compared by the log-rank test after splitting
patients at the median predicted risk within each cohort.
Split~3 contains only 17 test patients (8 IR/HR, and 3 positives each
for D19 MRD, D46 MRD and relapse), so its estimates are indicative
only.
No correction was made for multiple comparisons; all $p$-values are
two-sided.
