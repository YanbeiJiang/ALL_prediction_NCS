\abstract{%
Prediction models for paediatric acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL)
are usually trained on a hospital's own historical cases.
A hospital that adopts a new protocol has none, and the diagnostic
marrow smear, examined in every patient, rarely enters such models.
We asked how much local data a large language model (LLM) is worth
along the clinical timeline, and what automatically measured marrow
morphology adds.
In 301 patients split by enrolment year, a routine external cohort of
20 patients and an external cohort of 21 patients who all relapsed, we
predicted Final Risk, day-19 and day-46 minimal residual disease (MRD),
relapse, event-free survival (EFS) and overall survival (OS) from six
timeline-ordered feature sets available before any MRD assessment.
Cell size, cytoplasm, chromatin, nucleoli and vacuoles were measured
automatically on the Day-1 smear.
The LLMs received these measures as a short text report, without
images, and random forests and random survival forests received them
as variables.
An equal-weight ensemble of five LLMs matched random forests trained on
about 50 local patients for Final Risk and 100 for day-19 MRD.
Morphology improved the ensemble for all six targets.
With it, the ensemble reached an AUROC of 0.95 for Final Risk and 0.81
for day-19 MRD, close to fully trained forests (0.95 and 0.84), and
C-indices of 0.71 for EFS and 0.72 for OS, whereas survival forests
stayed at or below 0.61 with or without morphology.
In the relapse cohort, both approaches flagged only a minority of the
children at diagnosis (29\% and 38\%).
The LLM ensemble still ordered the time to relapse and to death
(C-index 0.67 and 0.83), but the survival forests did not (0.40 and
0.57, $P<0.01$ and $P<0.05$).
LLMs given routine data and an automated morphology report can
therefore be used before local data accrue, and they remain
complementary to trained models afterwards.
}

\keywords{paediatric acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, large language

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%%==============================================================%%

Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL) is the most common childhood
cancer and accounts for about 25\% of paediatric
cancers~\cite{[CITE:Hunger2015]}.
Risk-adapted chemotherapy has raised five-year event-free survival
(EFS) above 90\% in high-income settings, but the 15--20\% of patients
who relapse do much worse, with long-term survival of 30--50\% after
re-induction~\cite{[CITE:Bhojwani2013],[CITE:Pui2008]}.
Most relapses occur after apparent remission, so identifying high-risk
patients early enough to change their therapy is still an open problem
in paediatric oncology.

Current risk stratification assigns Initial Risk at diagnosis and
Final Risk after the Day~46 induction assessment.
It relies on fixed cut-offs for age, white blood cell count,
cytogenetics, immunophenotype and minimal residual disease (MRD) that
were established in large cooperative-group
studies~\cite{[CITE:Schultz2007],[CITE:Borowitz2008],[CITE:Conter2010]}.
These rules do not give individual probabilities of relapse or
survival, and many studies have tried to provide them.
They include Cox-regression
nomograms~\cite{[CITE:Mao2020],[CITE:Zhang2020],[CITE:He2022],[CITE:Sang2026]},
risk scores and association studies built on the day-19 and day-46 MRD
time points of the CCCG-ALL-2015
protocol~\cite{[CITE:Liu2022TALL],[CITE:Chen2025FrontPediatr]},
machine-learning models on routine clinical
variables~\cite{[CITE:Pan2017],[CITE:Mehrbakhsh2024],[CITE:Song2025],[CITE:Cui2025Transformer],[CITE:Saravanan2026]},
and models built on gene-expression, single-cell, exome, methylation
or RNA-sequencing
profiles~\cite{[CITE:Kang2010],[CITE:Fitter2021],[CITE:Good2018],[CITE:Bohannan2022],[CITE:Mosquera2024],[CITE:Zhai2026]}.
Relapse has been the hardest target.
Computational flow cytometry at diagnosis did not predict
it~\cite{[CITE:Martinez2025]}, and the largest machine-learning relapse
model reached 68.5\% sensitivity at 50.3\%
specificity~\cite{[CITE:Saravanan2026]}.

Most of these models simplify the clinical setting in ways that matter
at the bedside (Table~\ref{tab:related}).
They usually predict one endpoint at one decision point and are
validated by random split or cross-validation.
A systematic review found external validation in only 5 of 20
machine-learning models for paediatric haematological
malignancies~\cite{[CITE:Gurumurthy2025]}, a meta-analysis of
childhood-cancer prognosis models found no study that had assessed
AI-based models in haematological malignancies~\cite{[CITE:Varga2024]},
and none of the paediatric ALL studies we identified split patients by
enrolment year.
The closest work to ours, clinTALL~\cite{[CITE:Stoiber2026]}, predicts
T-ALL subtypes, competing events and EFS jointly with a multi-task
network restricted to pre-induction variables.
It does not order features along the acquisition timeline and did not
evaluate outcome prediction quantitatively on its external cohorts.

In practice, clinical prediction is shaped by three timelines, and
existing paediatric ALL models address them only in part.
The first is the \emph{feature-acquisition timeline}.
A patient's data arrive in a fixed clinical order, with white blood
cell count and age at admission (Day~0), immunophenotype on Day~1, and
fusion genes, karyotype and FISH by Day~7--10, after which treatment
response is measured as MRD on Days~19 and 46.
A model trained on features pooled across all time points uses
information that is not yet available when the decision is made, and
its estimates are optimistic~\cite{[CITE:Steyerberg2019]}.
The second is the \emph{prediction-target timeline}.
Clinicians make a sequence of decisions, from early response (D19 MRD)
and end-of-induction response (D46 MRD) to Final Risk assignment and
long-term outcomes (relapse, EFS and overall survival, OS).
Studying one target at a time hides the fact that one
data-acquisition workflow has to support all six decisions.
The third is the \emph{data-accumulation timeline}.
A hospital's case database grows year by year, so a model deployed in
a given year can only have learned from patients enrolled before it.
Random train--test splits ignore this and cannot show how a model
behaves as the database grows.

The third timeline raises a practical problem.
A hospital that adopts a new protocol, or a centre that starts to
collect structured data, has no local cases to learn from.
Large language models (LLMs) have been used to predict cancer survival
from initial oncology consultation
documents~\cite{[CITE:Phaterpekar2026]}.
On adult hospital data, language models have predicted readmission,
mortality and other outcomes after domain pretraining, with temporal
and prospective validation~\cite{[CITE:Jiang2023NYUTron]}, or from
tokenised patient timelines~\cite{[CITE:Renc2024ETHOS]}, and features
extracted by LLMs from clinical notes have improved survival models in
adult cancer~\cite{[CITE:Kiermeyer2026]}.
With serialised structured data, the value of prompted LLMs depends on
the comparator.
They have often outperformed conventional models trained on ten
examples~\cite{[CITE:Zhu2026ClinicRealm]} but fallen well short of
fully trained ones~\cite{[CITE:ClinicalBench2026],[CITE:Brown2025JAMIA]}.
Retrieval of similar patients or of external knowledge can
help~\cite{[CITE:ChenPSB2026],[CITE:Datta2026],[CITE:Xu2024RAMEHR],[CITE:Zhu2024EMERGE],[CITE:Chen2025JMIR]},
but none of these studies concerns paediatric oncology.

A second source of information is examined in every patient but rarely
reaches a prediction model.
The diagnostic bone marrow smear is read on Day~1, and the size of the
blasts, the amount of cytoplasm, the nucleoli and the presence of
vacuoles once defined the FAB subtypes of ALL~\cite{[CITE:Bennett1976]}.
In current risk stratification the smear is used for diagnosis and
does not enter prediction as quantitative variables.
If the smear were turned into a few quantitative, interpretable
measures, both kinds of model could use it, a trained model as
variables and an LLM as a short report read alongside the clinical
data, without any image.

It is therefore unclear how much local data an LLM is worth, for which
decisions it is enough, whether it stays useful once a case database
has accrued or when a model is moved to another hospital, and whether
information missing from the structured record, such as marrow
morphology, adds to it.
We study these questions on a dataset in which six temporally ordered
clinical feature sets (five strictly nested, with $\mathcal{F}_4$ a
Day-1 branch of $\mathcal{F}_3$) cover the feature-acquisition timeline
up to the end of diagnostic work-up, six prediction targets cover the
target timeline, and enrolment year defines the data-accumulation
timeline.
Every target is predicted before the first MRD assessment, so MRD is
always an outcome and never an input.
We compare an ensemble of five LLMs (Phase~1) with decision trees,
random forests and random survival forests trained on the growing case
database under temporal splits (Phase~2).
We add an automated morphology profile of the Day-1 marrow smear to
both, and test both without retraining on two external cohorts, a
routine series and a series in which every patient relapsed.
Finally, a retrieval step uses the accumulated cases to revise model
probabilities and to ground clinical narratives written by an LLM.

We make four contributions.
(\textit{i})~A timeline-aligned evaluation of six clinical decisions in
paediatric ALL that shows when each becomes predictable before
treatment response is known.
Final Risk is largely fixed by Initial Risk at Day~5, day-19 MRD
becomes predictable once immunophenotype is available, and relapse and
day-46 MRD remain the hardest targets.
(\textit{ii})~An automated, image-free route from the diagnostic marrow
smear to prediction.
Measures of cell size, cytoplasm, chromatin, nucleoli and vacuoles,
passed to the LLMs as a short text report, improved the LLM ensemble
for all six targets.
(\textit{iii})~An estimate of what LLMs are worth relative to
accumulating local data.
The LLM ensemble matched random forests trained on about 50 and 100
local patients for Final Risk and day-19 MRD.
For EFS and OS, locally trained survival forests did not reach it at
any training size, with or without morphology, and in an external
cohort in which every patient relapsed the LLM ensemble ordered the
time to relapse and to death while the survival forests did not.
(\textit{iv})~A retrieval-based belief revision extended to censored
survival outcomes, with narratives written by an LLM for physician
review.

\subsection{Cohorts and timeline-aligned design}
\label{sec:cohort}

The internal cohort comprised 301 children with ALL treated at
[HOSPITAL NAME] between 2015 and 2019 (Table~\ref{tab:cohort}).
The six targets span the treatment timeline.
They are Day-19 MRD positivity (D19 MRD $\geq 1\%$), Day-46 MRD
positivity (D46 MRD $\geq 0.01\%$), Final Risk (LR versus IR/HR),
relapse, event-free survival (EFS, with relapse, induction failure or
death as events) and overall survival (OS).
Two external hospitals provided separate cohorts.
The Lianyungang cohort (Lianyungang First People's Hospital, 20
patients diagnosed 2019--2023) is a routine series with two relapses.
The Fudan relapse cohort (Children's Hospital of Fudan University,
Shanghai, 21 patients, 2017--2021) is a referral series in which every
patient relapsed.
We use it to ask whether relapse can be anticipated at diagnosis and
whether the predicted risk orders the time to relapse and to death
after relapse.
No external data were used for training.

Figure~\ref{fig:overview} summarises the design.
Six feature levels $\mathcal{F}_1$--$\mathcal{F}_6$ follow the order
in which data become available, from white blood cell count and age at
admission to fusion genes, karyotype and FISH at Day~7--10
(Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:featurelist}).
All six targets are predicted at the pre-specified decision level
$\mathcal{F}_6$, the last level available before the Day-19 MRD
assessment.
MRD is therefore never an input, and every prediction is made while
therapy could still be adapted.
At each level we compare an equal-weight ensemble of five LLMs
(GPT-6.1-Sol, Gemini-4-Argon, Gemma-4-31B-it, InternVL3.5-30B-A3B and
Qwen3.6-35B-A3B) with random forests (RF) and random survival forests
(RSF) trained on patients from earlier enrolment years.
The main internal test set is the 2018--19 enrolment cohort (Split~2,
with 186 training and 114 test patients).
The other two temporal splits are reported in Supplementary
Table~\ref{tab:supp_decision}.
Discrimination is measured by AUROC for classification and by
Harrell's C-index for survival.
For classification, the LLM ensemble's score is the fraction of the
five models that predict the positive class.

From $\mathcal{F}_3$ onward, each level can be combined with the
morphology of the Day-1 marrow smear ($+$M, Methods).
An automated pipeline segmented the nucleated cells on the smear
images, measured their size, cytoplasm, chromatin, nucleoli and
vacuoles, and summarised them as a morphology profile for each patient.
Profiles were available for 293 of the 300 internal patients in the
temporal splits and for 40 of the 41 external patients.
The LLMs received morphology as a short text report added to the
clinical features, never as images, and the trained models received
the same measures as extra variables.
Predictions with morphology also use only data available by
Day~7--10.
The same pipeline was applied to the external smears (Methods).

%%--------------------------------------------------------------%%
\subsection{What becomes predictable, and when}
\label{sec:when}
%%--------------------------------------------------------------%%

Figure~\ref{fig:when} shows discrimination for every target at every
feature level, in the internal test set and in the two external
cohorts.
We describe four findings from the internal test set and note where
the external cohorts agree.

Final Risk is largely fixed by Initial Risk.
The RF reached an AUROC of 0.87 from white blood cell count and age
alone and 0.95 once Initial Risk entered at $\mathcal{F}_5$, and the
LLM ensemble rose from 0.79 to 0.93 over the same levels.
The RF reached similar values in both external cohorts, and the LLM
ensemble somewhat lower ones.
This follows from how Final Risk is defined.
Initial Risk alone reproduced Final Risk in 90.7\% of internal
patients, and adding the two MRD thresholds of the protocol reproduced
it in 299 of 301.
At $\mathcal{F}_6$ neither approach did better than Initial Risk alone,
with balanced accuracies of about 0.90 for both.
What remains to be predicted is which initially low-risk patients will
be upgraded, and 26 of the 28 upgraded patients were MRD-positive.
Predicting Final Risk is therefore mostly a matter of predicting MRD.

D19 MRD becomes predictable with immunophenotype.
For the RF, the AUROC was at or below chance at $\mathcal{F}_1$ and
$\mathcal{F}_2$ and about 0.8 from $\mathcal{F}_3$, when
immunophenotype and flow cytometry markers entered.
The LLM ensemble improved at the same level and reached about 0.8 from
$\mathcal{F}_5$.
This could not be confirmed externally.
Lianyungang had no D19 MRD-positive patient, and in the Fudan cohort,
with four positive patients, both approaches stayed below 0.5 from
$\mathcal{F}_3$ onward.

Before MRD, survival discrimination comes mainly from the LLMs.
The RSF C-index stayed at or below 0.65 at every level.
The LLM ensemble reached 0.71 for OS once Initial Risk became
available at Day~5, and 0.68 for EFS when cytogenetics entered at
$\mathcal{F}_6$.
Without MRD, which dominates prognosis in current
protocols~\cite{[CITE:Borowitz2008],[CITE:Conter2010]}, the trained
models had few local events to learn from.
The Fudan relapse cohort showed the same pattern
(Section~\ref{sec:external}).
In Lianyungang, which had only three events, the RSF was ahead at most
levels.

Relapse and D46 MRD stay close to chance.
Neither approach exceeded an AUROC of 0.70 for either target at any
level.
The external cohorts could not test D46 MRD, and the Lianyungang
relapse estimates rest on two relapses.
Results for each LLM and each level are given in Supplementary
Tables~\ref{tab:phase1_full}--\ref{tab:phase2_ext}.

%%--------------------------------------------------------------%%
\subsection{Automated marrow morphology improves the LLM ensemble}
\label{sec:morph_results}
%%--------------------------------------------------------------%%

Adding morphology improved the LLM ensemble for all six targets in the
2018--19 test set (Table~\ref{tab:main}).
The largest gain was for D46 MRD (AUROC 0.60 to 0.67), the target that
clinical features alone left closest to chance.
Only the gain for EFS (C-index 0.68 to 0.71) had a confidence interval
that excluded zero.
The trained models gained little.
With morphology they stayed within 0.01 of their clinical-only values
for Final Risk, D19 MRD and OS and fell for the other three targets.
A forest has to learn the role of 16 added variables from 186 training
patients, whereas the LLMs read the same information as a short
report.

%%--------------------------------------------------------------%%
\subsection{How many local patients is an LLM worth?}
\label{sec:worth}
%%--------------------------------------------------------------%%

To measure the value of the LLMs in local patients, we retrained RF and
RSF on random subsets of patients from the 2015--17 training years and
tested them on the same 2018--19 patients as the LLM ensemble
(Fig.~\ref{fig:worth}).
The LLM ensemble matched trained models built from about 50 patients
for Final Risk and about 100 for D19 MRD.
For EFS and OS the RSF stayed below the LLM ensemble at every training
size, including all 186 training patients.

With all training data and with morphology added to both approaches
(Table~\ref{tab:main}), the LLM ensemble was level with the RF for
Final Risk and somewhat behind it for D19 MRD.
It was ahead of the RSF for EFS (C-index 0.71 versus 0.58, $p=0.050$)
and for OS (0.72 versus 0.61, not significant).
Without morphology the differences pointed the same way but none was
significant, and the survival differences also favoured the LLM
ensemble in the other temporal splits where they could be estimated
(Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:supp_decision}).
The OS training sets contained only 8 and 13 deaths, which leaves a
trained model little to learn from.

\subsection{External cohorts: a routine series and a relapse cohort}
\label{sec:external}
%%--------------------------------------------------------------%%

Applied without retraining to the 20 Lianyungang patients, both
approaches transferred for Final Risk and EFS (Table~\ref{tab:main},
Fig.~\ref{fig:external}a).
With two relapses, three EFS events and one death, this routine series
cannot test relapse, OS or MRD prediction.
It does show how the relapse predictions behave at another hospital.
The RF predicted relapse for 16 of the 18 patients who did not
relapse, and the LLM ensemble for 4.

All 21 Fudan patients relapsed, so this cohort asks whether a high
predicted risk at diagnosis identifies the children who will relapse
and whether it orders when they relapse and die.
The LLM ensemble flagged 6 of the 21 children (29\%) and the RF 8
(38\%), similar to their sensitivities in the internal test set
(Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:relapse_detection}).
Neither approach therefore identified most of these children at
diagnosis.
The LLM ensemble's predicted risk did, however, order the events.
Its C-index was 0.67 for the time to relapse and 0.83 for death after
relapse, against 0.40 and 0.57 for the RSF ($p=0.005$ and $p=0.028$).
Children placed above the median risk by the LLM ensemble relapsed
earlier (log-rank $p=0.037$, Fig.~\ref{fig:external}c), whereas the
RSF's higher-risk group tended to relapse later ($p=0.078$,
Fig.~\ref{fig:external}e).

Morphology profiles were obtained for 40 of the 41 external patients.
With morphology the LLM ensemble improved in seven of the eight
evaluable external comparisons (Supplementary
Table~\ref{tab:morph_config_ext}).
The survival forests gained more in the Fudan cohort, so with
morphology the LLM ensemble stayed ahead but no longer significantly.
With morphology the RF flagged 15 of the 21 Fudan children and the LLM
ensemble 7.
Because no Fudan patient was free of relapse, a higher detection rate
here cannot be told apart from flagging more patients in general.

\subsection{Retrieval-based belief revision and clinical narratives}
\label{sec:rag}
%%--------------------------------------------------------------%%

Revising RF probabilities towards the outcomes of the five most similar
training cases (Methods) changed the classification AUROC in both
directions, by at most about 0.07 in all test sets except the
17-patient 2019 test set (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:belief_revision}).
For survival, a censoring-aware variant that replaces neighbour
outcomes with a similarity-weighted Kaplan--Meier event probability
gave small but consistent gains (mean C-index change $+0.006$ over 48
comparisons, Wilcoxon $p=0.022$).
For each of the 17 patients in the 2019 test set and each
classification target, an LLM (Gemini-4-Argon) then wrote a short
narrative from the prediction, the decision-tree path and the
retrieved cases.
A structured physician evaluation of these narratives is under way
(Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:narrative_eval}).

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
%%==============================================================%%

Most prediction models in paediatric ALL assume that a hospital's own
historical cases are available for training.
When we aligned six clinical decisions with the timelines along which
data are acquired and accumulate, this assumption mattered less than
expected for several of them.
With every decision made at the end of diagnostic work-up, before any
MRD result, an ensemble of five LLMs matched random forests trained on
about 50 and 100 local patients for Final Risk and D19 MRD.
For EFS and OS, survival forests did not reach it with any available
number of local patients.
In an external cohort in which every patient relapsed, it ordered the
time to relapse and to death and the trained survival forests did not.
A short automated report of the Day-1 marrow smear, given to the LLMs
as text, improved the ensemble for every target and brought it level
with fully trained random forests for Final Risk.
The same measures added little to the trained models.

Prompted LLMs have outperformed conventional models trained on ten
examples~\cite{[CITE:Zhu2026ClinicRealm]} but fallen well short of
models trained on full
datasets~\cite{[CITE:ClinicalBench2026],[CITE:Brown2025JAMIA]}.
Our learning curves place the crossover within a single disease, at
about 50 patients for Final Risk and about 100 for D19 MRD, and beyond
the available data for EFS and OS, where the training sets held only
8 to 13 deaths.
The LLMs helped most where local events were scarce and where the case
mix shifted, as in a referral series of relapsed patients.
In both situations a locally trained model has little to learn from or
has learned from a different case mix.
A centre starting a new protocol could therefore use an LLM ensemble
with the automated morphology report for survival risk from the first
patient, and switch to or combine it with locally trained models for
the classification targets once 50 to 100 cases have accrued.

For the classification targets, the LLM ensemble had no advantage over
trained models once a modest case database existed, and for Final Risk
neither approach did better than Initial Risk alone.
Decisions that a stratification protocol defines are better
implemented as explicit rules than predicted by a language model.
The LLMs were useful instead for outcomes that no rule defines, mainly
survival and, to a lesser extent, early treatment response.

The marrow smear is examined in every patient, yet its content rarely
reaches a prediction model.
Giving it to the LLMs as a few measured statements rather than as
images has practical advantages.
Each statement can be checked against the smear.
Expressing each measure as a percentile within its imaging batch put
batches with different staining and image acquisition on a common
scale without retraining, and after rescaling to a common red-cell
diameter the pipeline also worked on smears from two other hospitals.
A text-only model can use the report for a few hundred tokens per
patient.
A forest, by contrast, has to learn the role of each added variable
from 186 training patients, which may explain why the same measures
helped the trained models little in the internal test set.
In the Fudan relapse cohort the survival forests gained more from
morphology than the LLM ensemble did, a result that rests on 21
patients and needs confirmation.

Because all predictions were made before treatment response was
measured, they show what can be anticipated from diagnostic data
alone.
In this setting the trained survival forests stayed at C-indices of
0.65 or below in the internal test set, in line with the established
role of MRD, which our design withholds, as the dominant prognostic
factor~\cite{[CITE:Borowitz2008],[CITE:Conter2010]}.
The LLM ensemble reached about 0.7.
Relapse and D46 MRD remained the hardest targets.
Internally they were near chance with clinical features at every level
and reached AUROCs of about 0.67 with morphology, in line with the
failure of computational flow cytometry at
diagnosis~\cite{[CITE:Martinez2025]} and the modest discrimination of a
large clinical relapse model~\cite{[CITE:Saravanan2026]}.
The Fudan relapse cohort points the same way.
Of 21 children who went on to relapse, the LLM ensemble flagged 6 and
the random forest 8 at diagnosis, although the LLM ensemble's predicted
risk did order when they relapsed.
Stronger relapse signal has so far come mainly from single-cell and
molecular profiles~\cite{[CITE:Good2018],[CITE:Mosquera2024]}, which our
routine feature set does not include.
Adding such data at the right point of the timeline is the next step.

Revising model probabilities with similar past cases gave small but
consistent gains for survival and mixed results for classification.
Retrieval helps when patients with similar features have similar
outcomes, and it adds noise when they do not, as for relapse.
Revising only when the neighbours' outcomes agree beyond a calibrated
threshold would be a simple safeguard.

This study has several limitations.
The internal cohort comes from a single institution, is smaller than
the cooperative-group and registry cohorts behind earlier risk
models~\cite{[CITE:Schultz2007],[CITE:He2022],[CITE:Sang2026]}, and was
analysed retrospectively.
Prospective deployment, as reported for a clinical language model in
adult care~\cite{[CITE:Jiang2023NYUTron]}, is a stronger test than our
temporal and external evaluation.
The external cohorts are small (20 and 21 patients).
The Lianyungang cohort has too few events to test relapse, OS or MRD
prediction.
The Fudan cohort, in which every patient relapsed, tests whether
relapse is flagged and whether event times are ordered, but not
whether patients who do not relapse are spared a false alarm.
The confidence intervals of the external differences are wide, and
larger, prospectively collected external cohorts are needed.
Internally, the survival advantage of the LLM ensemble did not reach
statistical significance in any single test set, and most of the
morphology gains have confidence intervals that include zero.
The morphology measures are approximate.
Cell types were not separated, so all nucleated cells on the
diagnostic smear were treated as the leukaemic population, and batch
differences in staining and imaging were handled by within-batch
percentiles rather than by stain normalisation.
The external smears were few (two images per patient in Lianyungang)
and were matched to the internal scale only by rescaling to a common
red-cell diameter, so the external morphology results are indicative
only.
Learning curves were estimated on one temporal split, the 2019 test
set contains only 17 patients, and no correction was made for multiple
comparisons.
All predictions were made before MRD assessment, and we did not study
how they should be updated once MRD results are known.
The LLMs were used with fixed prompts, and their outputs may change
with model versions.
The two proprietary models were queried with their providers' default
decoding settings and are therefore not strictly deterministic, and
fine-tuning or few-shot prompting might improve the classification
targets.
Because each LLM returns a hard label for classification, the
ensemble's classification score has only six levels, which limits its
AUROC.
Finally, physician evaluation of the LLM narratives is in progress and
will show whether they are useful beyond illustrative case studies.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}
%%==============================================================%%

\subsection{Datasets and ethics}\label{sec:dataset}

The internal cohort comprised $n=301$ patients diagnosed with ALL at
[HOSPITAL NAME] between 2015 and 2019.
All patients were treated with risk-stratified chemotherapy protocols
consistent with contemporary Chinese paediatric oncology guidelines.
Ethics approval was obtained from [ETHICS NUMBER], and all data were
de-identified before analysis.
Each patient record includes structured clinical data (demographics,
haematological indices, immunophenotype, 51-marker flow cytometry
panel, cytogenetics, MRD values) and digitised images of the diagnostic
(Day~1) bone marrow smear.

Two external hospitals provided 41 patients in a single file with a
recorded hospital field, and the two hospitals are analysed as separate
cohorts (Section~\ref{sec:ood}).
The Lianyungang cohort is a routine series of 20 patients from
Lianyungang First People's Hospital (2019--2023), two of whom relapsed.
The Fudan relapse cohort comprises 21 patients from Children's Hospital
of Fudan University, Shanghai (2017--2021), a referral series in which
every patient relapsed.
Ethics approval for the external cohorts: [ETHICS NUMBERS].
Each external record contains the same structured variables and
digitised images of the diagnostic marrow smear (two images per
patient in Lianyungang and 20 in Fudan).
No external data were used in any model training.

\subsection{Temporally-structured feature hierarchy}\label{sec:features}

Feature sets are temporally ordered and, except for the
$\mathcal{F}_4$ branch, form a strictly nested sequence
$\mathcal{F}_1 \subseteq \mathcal{F}_2 \subseteq \mathcal{F}_3
\subseteq \mathcal{F}_5 \subseteq \mathcal{F}_6$, in which each level
contains exactly the features available at the corresponding clinical
time point.
$\mathcal{F}_4$ branches from $\mathcal{F}_3$.
$\mathcal{F}_1$ (Day~0) contains WBC count and age.
$\mathcal{F}_2$ (Day~1) adds blast percentage, $\mathcal{F}_3$
(Day~1) immunophenotype and 51 flow cytometry markers, $\mathcal{F}_4$
(Day~1) non-risk clinical factors, $\mathcal{F}_5$ (Day~5) CNS status,
Day-5 blasts and Initial Risk, and $\mathcal{F}_6$ (Day~7--10) fusion
genes, karyotype and FISH.
The complete variable list for every level is given in Supplementary
Table~\ref{tab:featurelist}.
D19 and D46 MRD are prediction targets and are not used as input
features at any level.
All six prediction targets are evaluated on the same feature hierarchy.
Morphology features of the Day-1 marrow smear
(Section~\ref{sec:morph}) can be added to
$\mathcal{F}_3$--$\mathcal{F}_6$ ($\mathcal{F}_k$$+$M).
They are not used with $\mathcal{F}_1$ and $\mathcal{F}_2$, which
serve as minimal clinical baselines.

For Phase~2, categorical features were one-hot encoded (CNS status as
CNS2 or CNS3 versus CNS1) and numerical features were z-score
normalised with training-split statistics only:
\begin{equation}
z_{ij}^{(\text{test})} =
\frac{x_{ij}^{(\text{test})} - \mu_j^{(\text{train})}}
     {\sigma_j^{(\text{train})}},
\label{eq:zscore}
\end{equation}
with missing values imputed by training-split medians.

\subsection{Bone marrow morphology features}\label{sec:morph}

\textbf{Cell segmentation and selection.}
Candidate cells on each diagnostic smear image were segmented with
SAM~3~\cite{[CITE:SAM]} using the text prompt \textit{``dark stained
nucleated cell''} (mask score $\geq 0.5$) at the native image
resolution.
Cells were then selected relative to each image's own background in
CIELAB colour space, with the white point set to the median of the
non-cell pixels.
A mask was kept as a nucleated cell when its mean $b^*$ offset from the
background was below $-33$ or, in images whose red cells were pink,
more than 15 units below the red-cell mode.
Masks were removed when they touched the image border, lay within a
larger nucleated mask ($>70\%$ overlap), duplicated a higher-scoring
mask (intersection over union $>0.5$), were smaller than half the
red-cell area of the image (platelets, debris, bare nuclei) or had a
solidity below 0.85 (clumps and smudged cells).
Cell types were not separated.
Because the diagnostic marrow in ALL is dominated by blasts, all
retained nucleated cells were treated as the leukaemic population.

\textbf{Cell-level measures.}
Cell size was expressed as the cell diameter divided by the red-cell
diameter of the same image, so that red cells serve as an internal
ruler.
Nucleus and cytoplasm were separated by two-cluster $k$-means on the
$a^*$ and $b^*$ channels.
The cytoplasm cluster was accepted only if it was clearly less magenta
than the nucleus and lay along the cell edge, and otherwise the cell
was recorded as having no visible cytoplasm.
Chromatin texture was measured as fine-scale contrast (difference of
Gaussians) and as the coefficient of variation of $L^*$ within the
nucleus.
Vacuole-like and nucleolus-like structures were detected by white
top-hat filtering at two scales, as small near-background spots in the
cell and as wider pale spots inside the nucleus.
The outline was described by circularity, solidity, nuclear solidity,
aspect ratio and cytoplasm blueness.

\textbf{Patient-level profile.}
Cell-level measures were summarised per patient as medians, spreads
and proportions.
The morphology profile comprises 16 descriptors, namely median cell
size and its coefficient of variation, the fractions of large ($>2$
red-cell diameters) and small cells, the cytoplasm fraction and the
fraction of cells with visible cytoplasm, cytoplasm blueness, chromatin
contrast and variation, nuclear lightness, nuclear and cell solidity,
circularity, aspect ratio, and the fractions of cells with
nucleolus-like and vacuole-like structures (Supplementary
Table~\ref{tab:morph_features}).
The smears had been digitised in three batches that differ in staining
and image acquisition (the median nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio, for
example, ranged from 0.74 to 2.77 across batches), so each descriptor
was expressed as a percentile among the patients of the same batch.
No outcome information enters this step.
Patients with fewer than 20 measured cells received no profile.
The profile rests on 46,964 cells (median 135 per patient) and covers
293 of the 300 internal patients in the temporal splits.
The external smears were processed in the same way
(Section~\ref{sec:ood}).

\textbf{Morphology as model input.}
For the LLMs, morphology was appended to the serialised clinical
features as a short text block, never as an image.
The report states the number of cells and the resulting reliability,
the typical cell diameter in red-cell units and micrometres, the share
of large cells, the share of cells with a visible cytoplasm rim and
the share of vacuolated cells, each with its within-batch percentile
and the batch's typical value, and notes that the measurements are
automated and supportive only.
Patients without a profile were scored with the prompt of the same
level without morphology.
For RF and RSF, the 16 percentile descriptors and a missing-profile
indicator were appended to the level's variables and preprocessed as
above.

\subsection{Phase~1: LLM inference}\label{sec:llm_inference}

For classification tasks, each patient's features were serialised into
a key--value list and presented with a task-specific system prompt
requesting a bracketed label (e.g., \texttt{[[IR]]}/\texttt{[[LR]]}).
For survival risk scoring, the same serialised features were presented
with a prompt requesting a continuous event probability (0.0 to 1.0).
Five LLMs were used.
Two are proprietary models accessed through their providers' APIs,
GPT-6.1-Sol (OpenAI) and Gemini-4-Argon (Google), and three are
open-weight models, Gemma-4-31B-it, InternVL3.5-30B-A3B and
Qwen3.6-35B-A3B.
The open-weight models were served with vLLM~\cite{[CITE:vLLM]} at
temperature~$0$ (greedy decoding, with the thinking mode of Qwen3.6
disabled).
The proprietary models were queried with the providers' default
decoding settings (Gemini-4-Argon on 30 and 31 October 2026 and
GPT-6.1-Sol on 31 October 2026).
Because each classification output is a single hard label,
classification performance of a single LLM is summarised by balanced
accuracy, $\mathrm{BA}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathrm{TPR}+\mathrm{TNR})$, which
equals the AUROC of the binary prediction.
The five LLMs were combined into an ensemble with equal weights and no
tuning.
For classification, the ensemble score is the fraction of models
predicting the positive class (patients without a valid answer from any
model were excluded), and the ensemble label is positive when at least
three of the five models agree.
For survival, the ensemble score is the mean of the five predicted
event probabilities.
The mean within-cohort rank of the five scores was used as a
sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:supp_decision}).
Per-patient outputs were cached.
The cohort was scored for every combination of feature level
($\times 6$), task ($\times 6$) and model ($\times 5$), and at
$\mathcal{F}_3$--$\mathcal{F}_6$ also with the morphology report
(Section~\ref{sec:morph}).

\subsection{Phase~2: Decision tree and random forest}\label{sec:dt}

A decision tree (DT, \texttt{max\_depth}=4, \texttt{class\_weight}=%
`balanced', \texttt{criterion}=`gini', \texttt{random\_state}=42)
and a random forest (RF, $n_\text{estimators}=100$,
\texttt{class\_weight}=`balanced') were trained for each feature level
and temporal split, with and without the morphology descriptors
(Section~\ref{sec:morph}).
Three temporal splits defined by an enrolment-year threshold
$\mathcal{T}_s$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:splits}) prevent any temporal leakage.
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{D}_s^{\text{train}} = \{i : \tau_i \leq \mathcal{T}_s\},
\quad
\mathcal{D}_s^{\text{test}} = \{i : \tau_i > \mathcal{T}_s\}.
\label{eq:splits}
\end{equation}
Enrolment year $\tau_i$ is taken from the diagnostic smear date, and
one patient whose date could not be parsed is excluded from all splits.
Patients with a missing label for a task are excluded from that task's
training and test sets.
The primary metric was AUROC, and F1-macro and balanced accuracy were
secondary metrics.

\subsection{Phase~2: Random survival forest}\label{sec:rsf}

A random survival forest (RSF)~\cite{[CITE:Ishwaran2008]} with 100
trees (\texttt{random\_state}=42) was trained on the same temporal
splits as the classification models.
A single survival tree, the survival counterpart of the classification
DT, was trained under the same protocol.
EFS was the time from diagnosis to the first of relapse, induction
failure or death, and OS was the time to death.
Patients were censored at last follow-up.
Evaluation used Harrell's C-index (Eq.~\ref{eq:cindex}), the IPCW
time-dependent AUC (Eq.~\ref{eq:itauc}) at $t^*\in\{12,24,36\}$
months, and the log-rank test on Kaplan--Meier curves split at the
median risk score.

\subsection{External evaluation}\label{sec:ood}

For both external cohorts, all $n=301$ internal patients served as the
training set.
No site-specific fine-tuning or recalibration was performed.
DT, RF and RSF were trained on the full internal set with the same
preprocessing (Eq.~\ref{eq:zscore}), and the same prompts were applied
to the external patients.
Each cohort was evaluated on all of its patients with a label for the
target.
A target was evaluated by AUROC only when the cohort contained at least
two positive and two negative patients, and by C-index only with at
least two events.
This excludes D19 MRD (no positive patient), D46 MRD (one) and OS (one
death) in the Lianyungang cohort and D46 MRD (none) in the Fudan
relapse cohort.
Because every Fudan patient relapsed, relapse was evaluated there as a
detection rate, the share of patients predicted to relapse at the
decision level.
The thresholds were those of the balanced accuracy (at least three of
the five LLMs, or an RF probability at or above the training
prevalence), with Wilson 95\% intervals and an exact McNemar test for
the paired comparison.
The same rules applied to the internal 2018--19 test set give the
reference sensitivity and specificity.
In this cohort the EFS C-index measures how well the predicted risk
orders the time to relapse, and the OS C-index how well it orders death
after relapse.
For morphology, the pipeline of Section~\ref{sec:morph} was applied to
the external smears after the images of each hospital had been rescaled
so that their median red-cell diameter matched the internal median
(43.5 pixels).
Percentiles were computed within each hospital, which plays the role
of an imaging batch.

\subsection{Decision levels, learning curves and protocol baseline}\label{sec:comparison}

Before comparing methods, we fixed a single decision level for all
targets, $\mathcal{F}_6$, the last level before the Day-19 MRD
assessment.
D19 and D46 MRD are thus predicted before they are measured, and Final
Risk, relapse, EFS and OS are predicted before treatment response is
known, when the intensity of subsequent therapy could still be adapted.
Comparisons on clinical features use this level.
With morphology, the clinical features come from a level up to
$\mathcal{F}_6$, so these predictions also precede any MRD assessment.
All comparisons between the LLM ensemble and trained models use the
same test patients.
For balanced accuracy, RF probabilities were thresholded at the
training-set prevalence.
Learning curves were computed on Split~2.
For $m \in \{10, 20, 40, 60, 80, 120, 160\}$ and for the full training
set, RF or RSF models with the settings above were trained on $m$
patients drawn without replacement from the 2015--17 enrolment years
(20 repeats per $m$) and evaluated on the 2018--19 test patients with a
valid LLM prediction.
The LLM-equivalent training size is the value of $m$ at which the mean
learning curve first reaches the LLM ensemble, obtained by linear
interpolation between adjacent sizes.
As references for Final Risk we used Initial Risk alone (LR versus
IR/HR), which is available at $\mathcal{F}_5$, and a protocol rule that
assigned IR/HR when Initial Risk was IR or HR, D19 MRD was
$\geq 1\%$, or D46 MRD was $\geq 0.01\%$.
Because the rule requires MRD, it is not available at the decision
level and serves only to show how Final Risk is defined.

\subsection{RAG belief revision and LLM narration}\label{sec:rag_methods}

For test patient $i$, the $K=5$ most similar training cases were
retrieved by cosine similarity in the standardised feature space:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{N}(i) = \underset{j \in \mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}}
  {\arg\text{top-}K}\
  \frac{\mathbf{z}_i \cdot \mathbf{z}_j}
       {\|\mathbf{z}_i\|_2 \|\mathbf{z}_j\|_2}, \quad K = 5.
\label{eq:rag}
\end{equation}
The RAG evidence probability is
\begin{equation}
p_{\text{rag}} =
  \frac{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} s_j\, y_j}
       {\sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} s_j},
\label{eq:prag}
\end{equation}
where $s_j$ is the cosine similarity to neighbour $j$ and
$y_j \in \{0,1\}$ is the binary task outcome (or event indicator
for survival).
The revised probability is
\begin{equation}
p_{\text{rev}} = \alpha\, p_0 + (1-\alpha)\, p_{\text{rag}},
\qquad
\alpha = 1 - (1-\alpha_{\text{base}})\sqrt{\bar{s}},
\label{eq:prev}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_\text{base}=0.6$ and
$\bar{s} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} s_j$.
For survival, $p_0$ is the min-max normalised RSF risk score.

After belief revision, a prompt was submitted to Gemini-4-Argon
(provider default decoding settings, 6 November 2026) to generate a
clinical narrative of three to five sentences.
The prompt contained the patient's $\mathcal{F}_6$ features, the RF
probability before and after revision, the human-readable DT decision
path~\cite{[CITE:Rudin2019]}, the six most salient features (ranked by
$\text{importance}_j \times |z_{ij}|$) and the five retrieved case
profiles with their outcomes.
Narratives were generated for every patient in the 2019 test set
(Split~3, models trained on 2015--18 at $\mathcal{F}_6$) and each
classification target.
Patient identifiers were not sent to the model.

\subsection{Survival metrics}\label{sec:surv_methods}

Harrell's concordance index:
\begin{equation}
\hat{C} =
\frac{
  \sum_{i \neq j}
  \mathbf{1}[T_i < T_j]\,
  \mathbf{1}[\delta_i = 1]\,
  \mathbf{1}[\hat{r}_i > \hat{r}_j]
}{
  \sum_{i \neq j}
  \mathbf{1}[T_i < T_j]\,
  \mathbf{1}[\delta_i = 1]
}.
\label{eq:cindex}
\end{equation}
IPCW time-dependent AUC:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\text{AUC}}(t^*) =
\frac{
  \sum_{i:\,T_i \leq t^*,\,\delta_i=1}
  \sum_{j:\,T_j > t^*}
  \mathbf{1}[\hat{r}_i > \hat{r}_j]\,\hat{w}_i
}{
  \sum_{i:\,T_i \leq t^*,\,\delta_i=1}
  \sum_{j:\,T_j > t^*}
  \hat{w}_i
},
\label{eq:itauc}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{w}_i = 1/\hat{G}(T_i)^2$ and $\hat{G}(\cdot)$ is the
Kaplan--Meier censoring estimator~\cite{[CITE:Uno2011]}.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}

AUROC and balanced accuracy were computed with
\texttt{scikit-learn}~\cite{[CITE:sklearn]}, and C-index and IPCW AUC
with \texttt{scikit-survival}~\cite{[CITE:sksurv]}.
Confidence intervals are percentile intervals from 2,000 patient-level
bootstrap resamples.
Differences between the LLM ensemble and trained models, and between
each model with and without morphology, were computed on the same
resamples, with two-sided $p$-values given by twice the smaller tail of
the bootstrap distribution of the difference.
Kaplan--Meier curves were compared by the log-rank test after splitting
patients at the median predicted risk within each cohort.
Split~3 contains only 17 test patients (8 IR/HR, and 3 positives each
for D19 MRD, D46 MRD and relapse), so its estimates are indicative
only.
No correction was made for multiple comparisons, and all $p$-values
are two-sided.

\noindent\textbf{Morphology configurations.}
Table~\ref{tab:main} reports one morphology-augmented ($+$M) result
for each target and method.
Besides the profile described in Methods (here M3), two earlier
versions of the profile were computed from the same images.
M1 consists of 17 per-patient means of cell geometry,
nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio, chromatin texture, nucleoli and the
fractions of hand-mirror and vacuolated cells (9,501 cells, profiles
for 219 of the 300 patients), and its report lists the 17 values.
M2 consists of 10 within-batch percentiles of cell size and shape,
nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio, nuclear and cytoplasm colour and size
heterogeneity (19,108 cells, 221 patients), and its report adds an
automated FAB-style impression.
For each target and method, the twelve combinations of clinical
feature level ($\mathcal{F}_3$--$\mathcal{F}_6$) and profile version
were evaluated on the 2018--19 test patients, and the combination with
the highest AUROC or C-index is reported (ties resolved in favour of
the later version and then the earlier level). The LLM ensemble uses
the same combination for all five models.
Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:morph_config} gives the chosen
configuration, the number of test patients with a profile of that
version, the same method's value with clinical features at
$\mathcal{F}_6$, and the difference between the two with its 95\%
bootstrap CI.
The same procedure was applied separately in each external cohort, on
all of its patients, with the detection rate as the criterion for
relapse in the Fudan cohort (Supplementary
Table~\ref{tab:morph_config_ext}).
On the external smears the M3 profile was available for 40 of the 41
patients, M1 for 13 and M2 for 11. Patients without a profile of the
chosen version were scored without morphology.
